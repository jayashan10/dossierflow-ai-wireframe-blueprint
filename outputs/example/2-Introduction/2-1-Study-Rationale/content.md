# 2.1. Study Rationale

Atopic dermatitis (AD), also known as eczema, is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterized by intense pruritus, eczematous lesions, and significant cutaneous barrier dysfunction. The disease represents a substantial global health burden, affecting approximately 15-20% of children and 2-10% of adults worldwide, with increasing prevalence observed over the past several decades in both developed and developing nations.

## Disease Burden

Moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis imposes a profound multidimensional burden on affected individuals, healthcare systems, and society. Patients experience chronic, intractable pruritus that frequently disrupts sleep, impairs concentration, and diminishes overall quality of life. The visible nature of skin lesions often leads to stigmatization, social isolation, and psychological comorbidities including anxiety and depression. In pediatric populations, atopic dermatitis significantly impacts academic performance and social development, while in adults, the disease correlates with reduced work productivity and increased absenteeism.

The economic impact of atopic dermatitis is substantial, encompassing direct healthcare costs associated with frequent physician visits, hospitalizations for disease flares or secondary infections, and long-term use of topical and systemic therapies. Indirect costs related to reduced productivity and caregiving burden further amplify the societal impact. Despite advances in understanding disease pathophysiology, many patients with moderate-to-severe disease continue to experience inadequate symptom control and diminished quality of life.

## Unmet Medical Need

Current therapeutic options for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis include topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, systemic immunosuppressants, and biologic agents targeting type 2 inflammation pathways. However, significant limitations persist in the therapeutic armamentarium. Topical therapies, while effective for mild disease, often prove inadequate for moderate-to-severe cases due to poor adherence, concerns about long-term safety with corticosteroids, and incomplete symptom control. Systemic immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil require therapeutic drug monitoring, carry risks of organ toxicity with prolonged use, and exhibit variable efficacy profiles.

Biologic agents, particularly interleukin-4 receptor alpha (IL-4Rα) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) inhibitors, have demonstrated efficacy in subsets of patients; however, not all patients achieve adequate response, and loss of response over time has been observed. Additionally, biologic therapies require parenteral administration, are associated with high costs, and may increase susceptibility to certain infections. There remains a critical need for novel therapeutic approaches that offer:

- Enhanced efficacy across diverse patient phenotypes and endotypes
- Favorable safety profiles suitable for long-term administration
- Convenient administration routes to optimize adherence
- Rapid onset of action to address acute disease flares
- Durable responses that maintain disease control over extended periods
